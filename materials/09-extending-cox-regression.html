9  Two or more predictors

In the previous sections, we focused on log-rank tests for single categorical predictors, and Cox regression for single continuous predictors.

However, just like in a regular linear model, a Cox regression can include multiple predictors. We’ll explore what that looks like in this chapter.

9.2 Fitting models with 2+ predictors

The good news about Cox regression is, just like regular linear models, once you know the syntax for one predictor, it’s very quick and easy to add others!

Using our trusty hospital_stays dataset, let’s recreate our one-predictor Cox regression (with age as the single continuous predictor).

Then, let’s follow it up with a three-predictor model, where we include surgery and weekend_admission simultaneously alongside age.

(These two additional variables capture whether or not a patient received surgery, and whether they were admitted on a weekday versus a weekend.)

  • R

The syntax for adding additional predictors is exactly the same as with the lm function - we use a plus sign.

hospital_stays <- read_csv("data/hospital_stays.csv")

# Single continuous predictor
cox_hosp_age <- coxph(Surv(time, discharged) ~ age,
                      data = hospital_stays)

# Two predictors
cox_hosp_multi <- coxph(Surv(time, discharged) ~ age + surgery + weekend_admission,
                        data = hospital_stays)

What differences do we observe when we look at the two models?

The most obvious difference is that, in our multi-predictor model, we get information about age, surgery and weekend_admission in the output.

We also see that some of the numbers have changed slightly. For instance, the concordance value increases a bit when we add more predictors.

(This is actually expected - in the same way that \(R^2\) increases when you add more predictors in regular linear models.)

TipGood news!

It doesn’t matter what order you put the predictors into the model. Due to the way that the model is estimated, you’ll always get the same coefficients no matter what.

9.2.1 Interactions

Along with adding more main effects, we can also include interaction terms in our Cox regression model.

  • R

Once again, the syntax is exactly the same as with regular linear models.

cox_hosp_int <- coxph(Surv(time, discharged) ~ age * surgery + weekend_admission,
                        data = hospital_stays)

Okay - so now you know how to fit these models. But that’s the easy part.

How can we now evaluate and interpret the new models we’ve created?

9.3 Checking assumptions

The first step, as with any type of modelling, is to check whether the assumptions are met.

Our four assumptions for Cox regression from previous chapters still apply:

  • Independence
  • Non-informative censoring
  • Linearity (for continuous predictors)
  • Proportional hazards

We actually already considered the linearity assumption in a previous chapter for the age predictor. Since we haven’t added any other continuous predictors, we don’t need to construct any more Martingale residuals plots here.

We also already examined the proportional hazards assumption in a previous chapter for the age and surgery variables.

So, from here, all we really need to do is check is the proportional hazards assumption for the weekend_admission variable, and consider any possible sources of non-independence or informative censoring.

Proportional hazards: weekend_admission

Now, the Schoenfeld residuals for proportional hazards:

  • R
ggcoxdiagnostics(cox_hosp_int, type = "schoenfeld")
`geom_smooth()` using formula = 'y ~ x'

No obvious issues are jumping out!

Remember, for our categorical predictors, it’s usually also helpful (and easier) to examine the log-log curves. Here’s a reminder of how to do that for the weekend_admission variable:

  • R
surv_hosp_adm <- survfit(Surv(time, discharged) ~ weekend_admission,
                             data = hospital_stays)

plot(surv_hosp_adm, fun = "cloglog")

This looks pretty parallel to me. (You can also double-check what you think of the actual survival curves, too, if you want some practice.)

Possible sources of non-independence/informative censoring

Particularly with the introduction of the surgery and weekend_admission variables, we can consider a few different possible issues.

Problem Assumption affected
Patients operated on by the same surgeon/surgical teams may have more similar outcomes Independence
Patients admitted around the same time may have more similar ailments and therefore more similar outcomes (e.g., drinking-related injuries are more common on weekends) Independence
Patients requiring complex or specialist surgeries may be more likely to be transferred to other hospitals, and thus lost from the study Non-informative censoring
Patients admitted at the weekend are more likely to be transferred to another hospital due to low/insufficient staffing, and thus lost from the study Non-informative censoring

Perhaps none of these affect our dataset, and from here, we’ll assume that they don’t. These are just some examples to continue to get you used to thinking about these assumptions.

9.4 Summary

While adding additional terms to the model doesn’t make it much harder to fit the model itself, it does mean a more lengthy assumption-checking and interpretation procedure.

TipKey Points
  • Fitting Cox regression models with 2+ predictors (including interactions) follows the same simple syntax as fitting linear models with multiple predictors
  • Assumptions of proportional hazards and linearity are checked for each predictor individually/one by one, even when there are multiple predictors
